# Preface — Disciplining Classics / Altertumswissenschaft als Beruf

The articles collected in the present volume explore a few aspects of the role which has been played within the recent history of various kinds of Classical scholarship by three processes of considerable general importance in the sociology of knowledge: disciplinization, institutionalization, and professionalization. To be sure, for certain features of these three processes there were ample pre-modern and early modern partial precedents and parallels, in Europe and elsewhere; but it has been the establishment, the stabilization, and above all the systematic interrelation of all three of them which have so deeply transformed the study of classical antiquity and of other fields over the past several centuries in Europe and America, and more recently in the other parts of the world which have been more or less profoundly influenced by the Western model. Though all three processes are closely related, particularly as they have been put into effect in the past two centuries, nonetheless each term picks out a somewhat different set of features within a complex set of developments and they can be distinguished conceptually.

Disciplinization denotes the establishment of a defined field of objects of knowledge and of a set of methods designed to study it in a regulated and inter-subjectively verifiable manner, in such a way as both to create a stable body of information which can be exchanged, examined, and modified synchronically among contemporary researchers on that field and to insure its diachronic transmission to future generations of researchers. Kant’s distinction between a “Wissenschaft” as a systematically interrelated body of knowledge on the one hand and any mere aggregate of unrelated information on the other played a key role in the imposition of disciplinarity upon certain earlier methods of study at the turn of the 19th century and remains enormously, if subliminally, influential. Disciplinization is above all methodological in nature and involves a set of related sub-processes, such as abstraction, formalization, specification, and generalization.

Institutionalization refers to the creation of trans-personal organizational systems for the furtherance of specified goals (such as the study of these disciplines) which are taken to be of sufficient importance for society as a whole to justify a substantial financial subvention which will benefit not only in the short term the specific individuals who participate in them but also in the long term an uncertain but presumably larger number of unknown future participants and unknown possible recipients of prospective advantages. Institutionalization secures an at least minimally long-term duration of focused and hence hopefully productive collaboration by distinguishing between functions on the one hand and the individuals who come to fulfill them more or less well and thereby to transform them on the other hand. This is above all an administrative phenomenon and coordinates such sub-processes as legislation, recruitment, career definition, and segmentation.

Professionalization, finally, designates the set of transformative processes which individuals are made to undergo so as to make them appear suitable for fulfilling the functions out of which these institutions are composed. Some tribes have rites of passage, others have Promotionen and Habilitationen, dissertations or théses. Before bestowing upon certain already mature adults the reward of a life-long sinecure, society takes a final opportunity to humiliate them by obliging them to fulfil requirements which, however sophisticated their scientific justification, also serve to remind the candidates, who are on the point of joining the bosses, that, however high the value that they themselves may have assigned to their merits, these alone would never have guaranteed them success: the final kick in the pants not only provides an additional impetus for the movement upwards but will also leave psychic bruises which the new boss will alleviate by distributing kicks of his own upon those who are at first only slightly younger than he is. This is above all an anthropological and psychological phenomenon and links with one another procedures of regulation, evaluation, and adjudication, definitions of competence and pertinence, and distinctions between sometimes finely shaded degrees of public and private.

The transformation in Classical studies between the end of the 18th and the beginning of the 19th century was one of the most spectacular, successful, and influential of the many processes of scientific modernization which have been determined by these three processes over the past several centuries. The story of the gradual propagation of modern methods and institutions of Classical philology from the seminars of Göttingen in the mid-18th century, through the courses of Friedrich August Wolf at the end of that century, to the establishment of the University of Berlin and the Humanistic Gymnasium in Prussia at the beginning of the next century, and from there (as well as from other sources, including Bavaria) to the rest of Germany, and, with some resistance, to Europe and the world, is a remarkable one. This story usually used to be told (and sometimes still is) in progressivist, unquestioningly positive, indeed occasionally almost hagiographic terms.! Now matters are changing. The crisis in which the field of Classics has found itself for a long time (in some senses, since the beginning) has led to wide-spread confusion, consternation, and complaint, but also, in at least a few cases, to a more sober analysis of the forces which supported and resisted and the mechanisms that rendered possible the transformation to a development which was, precisely, not inevitable and not even very likely. Recent studies of the disciplinization, institutionalization, and professionalization of modern scientific methods in Germany,” England,3 and France‘ have either focused upon the paradigm of Classical philology as a particularly rich and exemplary case or at the very least have important implications for the future study of the history of that field. Wedding the sociology of knowledge to the history of philological method, Pierre Bourdieu to Rudolf Pfeiffer, may tax the imaginative resources of even the most committed interdisciplinarist; but as the fruit from such a union might well issue forth new and more convincing ways of conceiving the history of Classical studies within their social, educational, and scientific contexts.

Instead of emphasizing the central steps of the transformation of modern Classical studies in Germany at the turn of the 19th century, which have been studied often (though not definitively), the essays gathered in this volume illuminate certain less familiar aspects of these general trends. A first group of three essays considers some particularly interesting examples of tendencies in pre-modern Classical studies which pointed in certain ways towards later changes but which at the same time indicate the resistances against which the institutionalization of the field had, has had, and has to struggle. A second group of five essays examines the crisis in German Altertumswissenschaft during the last third of the 19th century signalled by the criticisms of Friedrich Nietzsche, and broadens the scope of inquiry to include other developments at the turn of the 20th century. A final group of three essays analyses the question of the disciplinarity of Classics as it is posed in the immediate present and makes suggestions concerning future prospects and challenges. Thus the temporal span of this volume reaches from the 17th century in France to our own days and the foreseeable future. In terms of scholarly disciplines, the emphasis in this, as in the preceding volumes of this series, is upon the university practice of Classical philology; but additional light is thrown upon this field by consideration of related issues in European and American school education, papyrology, and cultural studies, and in the role of philology in the modernization of China. It is particularly worth noting that in this volume, devoted to the professionalization of Classical studies, more than half of the essays have been written by young scholars who stand at the beginning of their own professional careers.

This volume is derived from a conference on the subject of the professionalization and institutionalization of philology which was held in conjunction with the Internationales Wissenschaftsforum at Heidelberg University from 2-4 July 1999. As in the previous conferences in this series, which led to the publication of the volumes Collecting Fragments — Fragmente sammeln. Aporemata 1 in 1997, Editing Texts — Texte edieren. Aporemata 2 in 1998, Commentaries — Kommentare. Aporemata 4 in 1999, and Historicization — Historisierung. Aporemata 5 in 2001, the purpose was to explore historical and theoretical aspects of a scholarly practice which has played, and continues to play, an important role in the study of ancient Greece and Rome, as well as of many other cultures. This is the final volume in that series.

Although this volume is derived from a conference, it does not simply reproduce the papers presented there: all have been modified, some have been added, others have been omitted. It is hoped that the resulting volume will display even more coherence and unity than the colloquium did, while retaining something of its liveliness and diversity. Besides the authors whose articles are published here, the following scholars also participated in the discussions: Daniel Boyarin (Berkeley), Lorraine Daston (Berlin), Carlotta Dionisotti (London), Luca Giuliani (Munich), Anthony Grafton (Princeton), Andreas Kablitz (Cologne), Stefan Maul (Heidelberg). Funding for this conference, as for all of its predecessors, was provided by a Leibniz prize of the Deutsche Forschungsgemeinschaft; in the present case, additional support was provided by the Fritz Thyssen Stiftung (Cologne) and by the Stiftung Universitét Heidelberg. The staff of the Internationales Wissenschaftsforum, especially Dr. Theresa Reiter and Gudrun Strehlow, were uniformly friendly, helpful, and unruffled. The administrative organization of the conference was carried out with typical patience and inventiveness by Dr. Helga Kohler. The editorial work on this volume, including the preparation of the indices (but not their lemmatization, for which I must take the blame), was performed with grace, skill, and infinite good humor by Ingeborg von Appen; she was assisted in many ways by Dr. Manuel Baumbach and Dagmar Schweinfurth.

At the conclusion of this lengthy, intense, and (at least for me personally) highly satisfying project, I would like now once again, and for a last time, to express my heartfelt gratitude to the many students, friends, colleagues, and associates who have been involved in it for their generous and helpful contributions.

Florence, August 2001

Glenn W. Most
