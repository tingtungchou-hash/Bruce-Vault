## 核心论点
> [[Flower_2014_Consensus & Community in Republican Rome.pdf#page=5&selection=47,0,52,46|〇]] In this essay I am posing the following question: how should we understand republican politics in ancient Rome?

That is, through the concept of *consensus*, which emphasises the internal cohesion an agreement.

## 关键证据

### Epigraphic Sources
- **CIL 1^2 8-9 = ILLRP 310 = ILS 1 and 2**: Epitaph of L. Cornelius Scipio (cos. 259 BC), from the Tomb of the Scipios on the Via Appia. Contains the phrase *honc oino ploirume cosentiont* ("most Romans agree that this one man was the best of the good men"), attesting to consensus vocabulary in the 3rd century BC.
- **CIL 1^2 581 = ILS 18 = ILLRP 511**: *Senatus consultum de Bacchanalibus* (186 BC), discovered at Tiriolo in Bruttium. Mentions a quorum of 100 senators; the oldest extant *senatus consultum*.
- **RDGE 14, 75-76**: *Consilium* of the consul L. Calpurnius Piso (112 BC), listing members of the advisory council.
- **RDGE 12, S.C. de agro pergameno**: *Consilium* of a praetor (c. 101 BC), with 55 members listed in order of rank, two-thirds of lower rank, possibly including non-senators.
- **RDGE 23**: *Consilium* of the consuls M. Terentius Varro Lucullus and C. Cassius Longinus (73 BC), with 15 senatorial members; also references earlier *consilia* of Sulla in the 80s BC.
- **Augustus, Res Gestae Divi Augusti (RGDA) 34.1**: *per consensum universorum potens rerum omnium* ("by everyone's agreement having power over everything") --- Augustus claims consensus as the foundation of his power after Actium; the Greek version uses *euchai* ("prayers") rather than *homologia*.
- **Augustus, Res Gestae Divi Augusti (RGDA) 25.2**: Oath of allegiance to Octavian in 32 BC (*tota Italia sua sponte*).

### Papyrus
- **Claudius' Speech to the Senate (mid-1st century AD)** = Smallwood (1967) no. 367 = FIRA 1,44 = Bruns^7 53: The emperor Claudius rebukes senators for merely saying *adsentior* ("I approve") instead of giving their own opinions, a passage that serves as the lecture's epigraph.

### Literary Sources: Cicero
- **Cicero, De republica** (written 54--51 BC, set in 129 BC): Central to the theoretical discussion of the *res publica*; cited for the metaphor of the fading painting (5.2) used as the book's epigraph in *Roman Republics* (2010).
- **Cicero, De legibus** (begun 52 BC, unfinished): Companion work to *De republica*, conceived in the turbulent late 50s BC.
- **Cicero, Ad Atticum 1.19.2-3**: On the use of the lot (*sortitio*) in Roman politics.
- **Cicero, Ad Atticum 1.14.5**: Records a senate vote of 400:15 (February 61 BC).
- **Cicero, Red. Sen. 26**: Records a senate vote of 416:1 (July 57 BC).
- **Cicero, QFr. 2.1.1.3**: Additional evidence on senatorial procedure.
- **Cicero, Mil. 12**: Additional evidence on senatorial procedure.
- **Cicero, Phil. 10.2**: Additional evidence on senatorial procedure.
- **Cicero, De amicitia 96**: Two examples of bills rejected in the voting assemblies.
- **Cicero, De officiis 2.73**: Evidence of bills rejected by voters.

### Literary Sources: Livy
- **Livy 1.49**: Tarquin the Proud demonstrating autocratic rule by not consulting the senate.
- **Livy 5.30.7; 5.55.2; 6.39.2; 8.37.11; 27.21.4; 31.6-8.2**: Ten recorded instances of Roman voting assemblies rejecting a bill or a decision to go to war.
- **Livy, Periochae 49**: Further example of rejection in the assemblies.
- **Livy 29.14.8**: The apparently harmonious choice of the young Scipio Nasica as *optimus vir*.

### Literary Sources: Other Authors
- **Sallust, Bellum Catilinae 50--53**: The fullest surviving description of a senatorial debate (on the fate of the Catilinarian conspirators), though rewritten for historiographical purposes some twenty years after the event.
- **Appian, Bellum Civile 2.30**: Records a senate vote of 370:22 (December 50 BC).
- **Plutarch, Tiberius Gracchus 8**: Gaius Laelius (cos. 140 BC) gained the *cognomen* Sapiens after withdrawing an initiative for land reform.
- **Polybius 6.19**: A young Roman was expected to serve ten campaigns in the army before standing for political office.
- **Aulus Gellius, Noctes Atticae 3.18**: Mentions the obscure term *pedarius* for low-ranking senators.
