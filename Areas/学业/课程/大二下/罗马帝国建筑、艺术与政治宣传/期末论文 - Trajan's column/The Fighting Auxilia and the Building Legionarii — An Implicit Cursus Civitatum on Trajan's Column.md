**THE FIGHTING AUXILIA AND THE BUILDING LEGIONARII: AN IMPLICIT CURSUS CIVITATUM ON TRAJAN'S COLUMN**

Bruce (周星宇) PKU Department of History 2500014572

**Prelude**

My personal engagement with Trajan's Column started when I browse through it and observed that two categories of soldiers, namely legionarii and auxilia, were featured in a counter-intuitive way; Always taken as a military monument, Trajan's Column nevertheless didn't feature legionarii as fighters as frequently as we might assume. Instead, they were featured more as engaged in construction and marching, with a fair share of fighting actually taken by auxilia.

I. Introduction: auxilia vs. legionaries and a cursus civitatum

Trajan's Column took quite a counter-intuitive way of representing auxilia and legionarii, in that auxilia more often engaged in battles and legionarii more often engaged in construction. Since multiple researches have focused on a contrast between the Roman army and Dacians, emphasising the construction of a dichotomy of civilisation versus barbarism on Trajan's Column, this observation indicates a division of status among the Roman army, and render us different insights into the representation and construction of civilisation on Trajan's Column: Through an in-depth analysis both visual and quantitative of the different types of soldiers on the Roman side, I try to suggest a hierarchical instead of dichotomic perception of civilisation on Trajan's Column, arguing that Trajan's Column actually constructed what I have termed a cursus civitatum, a hierarchical system instead of a dichotomic one, and to explore its possible implications.

II. Quantitative assessment of the Roman army on Trajan's Column

The special design of Trajan's Column has long been a topic of scholarly debate. The spiral narrative it adopted necessitate that either the viewer went around it on neighbouring viewing platforms, or accept a degraded vertical axis reading. (Lehmann-Hartleben 1926, Gauer 1977, Coarelli 2000, Brilliant, 1984:90-4, quoted in [[Areas/学业/文献库/罗马艺术与视觉文化/Trajan's column/Trajan Fox 2019 Trajanic Trees on the column.pdf|Fox 2019]]:53; cf. Richard 1935) Either way was bound to disturb the continuity of the narrative, and it was plainly impossible for the viewers, especially those without a beforehand comprehension of the unfolding of the Dacian war, to follow the narrative closely and figure out what was going on. It was both logical and plausible then that the readers were inclined to view the column thematically instead of rigidly continuously.

And this was not necessarily a design flaw. The thematic approach Trajan's Column took was actually quite effective, and was followed by later commissioners of like monuments, Marcus Aurelius being an example. (Davies 1997:58) The point is, being a monument, be it military or funeral, the scattered nature of the scenes on Trajan's Column encouraged the viewers to approach it more directly, focusing on its pictorial grandeur instead of petit narrative details, thus reaching a broader audience.

That made it possible and necessary to conduct a quantitative assessment of Trajan's Column, paying first more attention to the structural arrangement of the figures instead of its details. Though the task division between auxilia and legionarii wasn't unnoticed, but it hasn't been approached with more caution and statistically affirmed. (Coulston 2003:403) This quantitative assessment thereby helps add to the certainty with which the conclusion was reached.

Since the focus of this article is to explore the civilisation versus barbarism on Trajan's Column, the categorisation was based on citizenship, dividing the Roman army into legionarii and (crudely speaking) auxilia. Legionarii were identified with loricae segmentatae and rectangular scuta, while auxilia were identified with loricae hamatae and round parmae, or, being specialist soldiers, otherwise identified, like with lorica squamata and bows typical of eastern archers. (cf. [[Areas/学业/文献库/罗马艺术与视觉文化/Trajan's column/COULSTON-MILITARYIDENTITYPERSONAL-2004.pdf|Coulston 2004]]:143ff.) To be sure, actual Roman army doesn't follow such division closely, on which [[Areas/学业/文献库/罗马艺术与视觉文化/Trajan's column/Charles-FlavioTrajanicmiles-2002.pdf|Charles (2002)]] gave a detailed account, but as an artistic representation, the army on Trajan's Column should be approached in accordance with their visual traits. When soldiers wearing loricae hamatae appeared in flocks together, the safer assumption is that they were meant all to represent auxilia, instead of legionarii, or the mix of the two.

Based on such categorisation, the numeral data of legionarii and auxilia (fig. 1), the advantage comparison between them (fig. 2) are as such. And if we focus on the irregular and unbalanced parts of the line-chart and put scene types into consideration, it soon became clear that the arrangement of auxilia and legionarii was not at random. Battle scenes clustered around the summits of the number of auxilia, while construction scenes generally accorded with summits of the number of legionarii;

Fig. 1 number of auxilia and legionarii by scene

Fig. 2 advantage comparison by scene with battle and construction scenes highlighted

Further statistical analysis attested a correlation between legionarii and construction, as well as auxilia and fighting.

Based on Point-Biserial Correlation analysis and Mann-Whitney U test (table 1), it shows a strong and robust positive correlation between auxilia and Battle scenes.[[1]](#_ftn1) Besides, though the correlation between legionarii and Construction scenes is weaker, manifesting only as a distribution difference rather than a linear relationship, it could be accounted with Legionarii also appear frequently in Battle scenes. Scene-by-scene counts and overall distribution of legionarii and auxilia during construction and battle activities (fig. 3) also showed a distinct division of labour between the two across the narrative scenes.

Thus it can be concluded that the difference between the share of responsibility between legionarii and auxilia was not conjectural or speculative. This quantitative assertion can be further enhanced and given greater clarity by in-depth imagery analysis of the respective representations of auxilia and legionarii.

III. A closer look: legionarii and auxilia in action

This section looks into xx groups of scenes, following a down-to-up sequence, and try to reinforce the argument indicated by quantitative examination and provide analysis with a visual dimension.

1. The orderly legionarii

Take scenes XI - XII (fig. 4) as an example. It showed the first group of construction scenes. Almost all engaged in construction were legionarii: They were all shown with lorica segmentata and in pairs. Digging the earth, conveying blocks, inserting logs, legionarii were featured in a cooperative and disciplined way, woven into a delicate representation of construction.

Fig. 4 Scenes XI - XII

There has been a debate on whether the building material used by legionarii was turves or stones. Richmond suggested that turves and timber while Coulston and Lehmann had it otherwise; Thill (2010) gave a detailed interpretation based on statistics and imagery analysis, arguing for the stone hypothesis, and stressing the stone structures' role in constructing the Roman self-image as being civilised. The contrast between wooden Dacian structures and stone Roman structures, considered together with the characterisation of Roman civilisation and Dacian savage, is especially convincing. And, with the Roman structures appearing frequently by the side of legionarii constructing them, it would be too strange to suggest turves and timber as major building material, which barely conveys a positive message and was bound to cause undesirable confusion among viewers. Such being the case, precisely as Thill's "civilisation under construction", the legionarii, working in a disciplined manner, handing blocks of buildings and inserting pillars, constructing (in the concrete as well as abstract sense of this word) civilisation with their own hands. (cf. [[Areas/学业/文献库/罗马艺术与视觉文化/Trajan's column/Trajan's column Building Trajan's column Lynne Lancaster.pdf|Lancaster 1999]])

Fig. 5 Scenes XV

Another part of construction was also done by the legionarii, i.e. clearing the trees. Since the major building material of the Roman army was stones, felling the trees and deforesting the lands of Dacians was more of "a war on the landscape". ([[Areas/学业/文献库/罗马艺术与视觉文化/Trajan's column/Trajan Fox 2019 Trajanic Trees on the column.pdf|Fox 2019]]:66f.) Trees, as a symbol attributed divinity by a lot of non-Roman populations across the frontier, had already been talked about. Lucan (Pharsalia, 3. 399-452) depicted a sacred grove adorned with "savage rites", "hideous offerings" and "human gore". Tacitus (Annales 14.30) recorded Romans felling the sacred trees "of savage superstitions". At the very least, three Roman legions were buried amid the woods of Teutoburg. Trees and forests were a deity among multiple non-Roman populations like several German or Celtic tribes, and the rites revolving around them were a source of Roman disgust and fear, and gradually so became the trees themselves, especially those pertaining to barbarian inhabitation. Legionarii clearing the forest thus represented a war on Dacian land and identity, a spectacle of taming and conquering; And the felling of trees and clearing of lands weren't sheer destruction, but part and parcel of construction, an imperative procedure of civilising and urbanising, as could be seen from scene XV (fig. 5), where the clearing of woods were shown with transporting timber to the construction scene. Such representation of tasks shouldered by the legionarii, in an disciplined and organised manner and often under Trajan's direct supervision, is indeed a "civilisation under construction", in both meanings of the word. (Davies 1997:62f.)

2. The fighting auxilia: barbarism or virtus?

The first pivotal point of representation of auxilia is that they took up almost most of the tasks of fighting. In the first battle with the Dacians (fig. 6), all the legionarii stayed in reserve and kept from fighting, while the auxilia joined battle. Auxilia showed great valour in battle, fighting with hastae and gladii.

Fig. 6 scene XXIV

Several soldiers showed severed heads to Trajan, and one soldier held a head by its hair in his mouth, a bare-chest, trousered warrior by his side, fighting with a club. The first impression such representation left is sheer savagery. Beheading and display of severed heads were part and parcel of barbarian rites and rituals, towards which the Romans showed more disgust than admiration. Diodorus Siculus (5.29.4-5) recorded Gauls beheading the enemies and keeping their skulls as souvenirs.[[2]](#_ftn2) Tacitus also talked about Germans nailing the skulls of fallen Romans of Varus' legions onto trunks.

Fig. 7 Dacian trophies of severed heads, scene XXV

But of course, beheading the enemies was not a privilege of uncultured barbarians. The Romans themselves had long been acquainted with beheading the criminals and enemies, and they too openly displayed severed heads. Livy recorded consul C. Claudius ordered Hasdrubal's head to be tossed before the enemy outposts. (27.51) Severed heads were also proof kills which would bring substantive rewards -- and that they were showing them to Trajan also attests it. ([[Areas/学业/文献库/罗马艺术与视觉文化/Trajan's column/Trajan Captor of Decebalus.pdf|Speidel 1970]]:149ff.) A distinction in the treatment of severed heads -- between their immediate display and subsequent discard, as opposed to permanent exhibition (fig. 7) -- also served to differentiate the Roman auxilia from their Dacian enemies. Also, that they showed the severed head to Trajan himself spoke a lot. In scene LXVIII (fig. 8) and other scenes alike, it was auxilia who brought Dacian captives to Trajan. This attested the loyalty and service (and indirectly, virtus) of auxilia, justifying their shared pitch of romanitas.

Fig. 8 scene LXVIII

An ambiguity was contrived here: the auxilia both recalled blatant barbarism, and at the same time displayed virtus of a soldier in search of Trajan the imperator's recognition, which symbolised recognition of the sovereign Populus Romanus, at whose hand rested the privilege to bestow citizenship.

Fig. 9 scene XXIV

Another interesting detail is the Iuppiter right above two auxilia, throwing thunder in the posture as they thrusted their spears. (fig. 9) This isomorphism indicated a divine favour frequently alluded to in epics, and it was auxilia who enjoyed it. (cf. [[Areas/学业/文献库/罗马艺术与视觉文化/Trajan's column/Trajan Fox 2019 Trajanic Trees on the column.pdf|Fox 2019]]:58f.) It seemed that auxilia were depicted in a hybrid way, both savage and allowed a considerable degree of romanitas in the battlelines.

Fig. 10 scene LXVI

Though legionarii also appeared in battle scenes, they more often stayed in reserve, not engaged in frontline fighting. Scenes LXVI - LXX (fig. 10) gave an illuminating example. Though legionarii were also featured, they were kept again in reserve, stay close in formation or handling devices, while it was auxilia who actually assumed the task of fighting the Dacians, whose ethnic diversity was fascinating in this pack of scenes. Auxiliary infantrymen wearing loricae hamatae fought with gladii and clipei in the front lines, bare-chest warriors close to their back, slingers and eastern archers wearing their iconic loricae squamata behind.

Fig. 11 legionarii in testudo formation, scene LXXI

Legionarii turned up once in a later scene, but in testudo formation, (fig. 11) which was more of a defensive formation and formed a contrast to the way in which auxilia fought -- In battle scenes auxilia projected more valour or cruelty, while legionarii, if ever featured, were given emphasis because on their discipline and reservation -- And even such defensive engagement was rare, which couldn't be explained solely by military technicality, as I'll argue below.

3. Fighting together, building together and merging together

As could be seen, in a great part of the column, the Legionarii and auxilia shouldered quite different responsibilities, the former building, the latter fighting; But we have to caution against essentialism. The identity and representation were fluid, albeit sluggishly, when the spiral went upward.

Fig. 12 scene CXV

In scene CXV (fig. 12), we see all kinds of Roman military forces literally mingled together: legionarii, auxiliary infantrymen, and eastern archers, in an alternating arrangement. This was impressive compared with previous scenes where auxilia and legionarii were literally separated. When the Dacian campaigns drew towards its end -- and, for auxilia, the day of discharge and endowment of citizenship that were approaching -- the two categories of soldiers were depicted more and more close together in a mixed manner. This couldn't be explained solely to have been an authentic military depiction with legionarii and auxilia being inserted so evenly next to each other. It could be understood even with greater clarity when considered alongside the thematic approach of Trajan's Column and the problem of turves versus stones. (see above)

Fig. 13 classiarii clearing the woods, scene XCII

And, auxilia engaged in construction scenes as well. In scene (fig. 13) Classiarii, though technically not part of auxilia, but also composed of non-citizens and enjoyed inferior status in the army, also engaged in clearing the woods. Given the symbolic gravity of clearing the trees, that such task once fell on the shoulders of the most inferior Classiarii was again assuring of their changing role throughout the reliefs.

So, to sum up, what does such representation hint to us? For auxilia, they shouldered most of the fighting tasks. In battle scenes the depiction of them was mixed with ambiguity between savage and virtus which the conquerors of the Mediterranean themselves used to fight. Instead of a choice between the two, I would suggest a co-existence. Such complex perception of barbarians had long been a received tradition: Scythians were known as cruel, uncultured nomads drinking with skulls, but when compared with the Persians they became "noble savages", deserving due respect from the Greek. (Herodotus, Histories 4.64-65, 4.46) Similarly, the auxilia composed of non-citizens were seen as inferior to legionarii, but they, being comrades of the Romans and were trustworthy and courageous, though not always tender and cultured. The narrative didn't follow the Greek prototype closely though. With Classiarii engaged in construction and legionarii joined battle right next to auxilia, auxilia were represented as gradually integrated into the Roman way of fight, of construction, and ultimately of life for whom the Roman citizenship, the reason why many auxilia enrolled, awaits. As Tacitus described (Agricola 21), they who were seen as barbarians could actually to pursue Roman eloquence in brand-new togae. ("eloquentiam concupiscerent. inde etiam habitus nostri honor et frequens toga")

IV. Auxilia, legionarii, and a cursus civitatum

The implications of such representation are curious. Legionarii were appreciated by their discipline while auxilia for their valiant virtus in service for the empire, though tinted by a touch of savage. Trajan's Column shaped an imperial perception where legionarii were disciplined and professional, where auxilia kept loyal to Rome and earned their deserving civitas through fight and where the prospect of civilization awaits the Dacians, newly defeated and entered into the fides of the Roman people.

Through the contrast between auxilia and legionarii, and that between ordinary auxilia and barbarian specialists which the paper didn't look into, the hierarchical instead of dichotomic order of Trajan's Column was clearly revealed.

The hierarchical characteristic has been discerned across the Roman society, for a slave to be free, he had to climb up the ladder of servi - liberti - cives; for a citizen to assume public offices, he had to climb up the cursus honorum of quaestor - aedile - praetor - consul. This, if not a central characteristic of the Roman society, is at least a pivotal aspect of the perception of itself and of its identity system (Hölkeskamp 2020:47-50) -- and, borrowing the terminology of the cursus honorum, I termed such perception of civilisation and barbarism, of us and the other, as a "cursus civitatum". The distinction was not dual, but hierarchical, and open to those who met the required conditions and had the volition, though with considerable difficulty (i.e., survival of at least twenty-five years of demanding service).

And, this was not only an inherent part of Roman political culture, but also indicates a relationship to Trajan's own experience and the evolving way of governing the Roman empire. Precisely as Pliny said, Trajan "surpassed the renown of his clan with courage/virtue" (qui generis tui claritatem virtute superasti). As an emperor born in Spain who didn't enjoy much inherited fame from his clan (Cassius Dio, Roman History 68.4.1) had deep connections with provincial affairs and battles at frontiers, Trajan paid considerable on military affairs and his soldiers, and was indeed an imperator sui generis. (Bennett 1997:64-68) He knew auxilia and legionarii personally through his military tribunate and governorship at upper Germania, and he lived with them, (Pliny the younger, Panegyricus 16.5) fought with them. The column he commissioned was idealised depiction of the Roman army in action, with legionarii being ordered, building the forts of civilisation block by block, the auxilia fighting in service of the empire, and Dacians entered in to fides populi romani, about to be tamed and Romanised (which was literally synonymous to "civilised"). -- Thereby the entire population of the Roman empire was embedded in a single Column, together with the omnipresent hierarchy, a cursus civitatum, governing them.
