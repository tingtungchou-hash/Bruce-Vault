## 论文正文

Zhou Xingyu (Bruce), Department of History, Peking University 2500014572

From Iunius Brutus to Benito Mussolini, _fasces_ represents one of the most enduring semiotic legacies of Roman visual rhetoric, embodying a spectrum of meanings from civic solidarity to state-sanctioned violence. My personal engagement with this icon stems from an ironic movie named _Fascisti su Marte_ (2006), where fasces played an important role in the popular culture of Fascist Italy. My background in Roman history naturally led me into a historical examination of this icon, and what interests me is precisely the process of interpretive accretion of its meanings, and the manipulation and power behind this process.

In this presentation I investigate a ritual represented in political imaginary: the removal of axes from the bundle. I encountered it during my readings, and after analyzing its origins, iconographic manifestation, and political manipulation, I propose that though often taken as an inherited Republican ritual, it was in fact retrospectively constructed and subsequently instrumentalised by late Republican dynasts and Julio-Claudian rulers, as a visual language of political legitimacy.

###### I. Cicero and the Ritual of Removal of Axes

Pre-dating the Republic, the fasces served as an instrument of power of life and death. But such function itself didn't automatically make it a symbol of the republic; Rather, it was through ritualistic restrictions that it was legitimised. Cicero, in his _De Re Publica_, emphasises this distinction by attributing to the early Republic the practice of removing the axes from the bundle, ([[Areas/学业/文献库/罗马艺术与视觉文化/fasces/The Fasces A History of Ancient Romes Most Dangerous Political Symbol (T. Corey Brennan) (z-library.sk, 1lib.sk, z-lib.sk).pdf#page=31|Brennan, 2022, p.18]]) presenting it as a gesture of respect toward the _res publica_:

Itaque Publicola lege illa de provocatione perlata statim securis de fascibus demi iussit (Cic. Rep. 2.55)

But is it the case in the early republic or in Cicero's own time? An examination of coins and architectures prove beneficial.

###### II. Roman Republican Coins and Architectures Featuring Axed Fasces

In Roman numismatics, coinage functioned as a primary medium for self-promotion, designed to garner favour for the moneyers. The imagery they carried, we have to assume, was therefore intended to convey messages that would resonate positively with their intended audience.

On a denarius issued in 83BC (RRC 357/1), honouring Gaius Norbanus, consul of the same year, fasces appeared together with a grain-ear and a caduceus. With caduceus evoking Hermes the patron of commerce ([[Areas/学业/文献库/罗马艺术与视觉文化/fasces/Tyson-Caduceus-1932.pdf#page=5|Tyson, 1932, p.495]]) and the grain-ear, the coin built a context of a highly _popularis_ agenda: the supply and distribution of grains. Opposed to Venus Victrix propagated by Sulla, the Venus featured with a diadem asserted authority, order and prosperity ([[Areas/学业/文献库/罗马艺术与视觉文化/fasces/Luce-PoliticalPropagandaRoman-1968.pdf#page=14|Luce, 1968, p.37]]). Curiously enough, on the reverse, the fasces were axed. Were Cicero's account to be accepted, such axed fasces could evoke intimacy neither among the _optimates_ who barely survived the bloodshed of Marius' unconstitutional seizure of Rome, nor among the _populares_ mindful of the threatening return of Sulla and his legionaries. The imageries would be uncomprehensible.

Another denarius from 63BC (RRC 414/1) adds to the confusion: even in the consular year of Cicero himself, when Catilina was about to burn the city of Rome and put the axes to use, (Cic. _Catil_. 2.13) he didn't bother to rid the _fasces_ of their axes. How can we account for it? To note the chronology of Cicero's work might render us some help. Cicero wrote his _De Re Publica_ in the immediate aftermath of his exile and a time of approaching crises, and for this reason we shouldn't take the testimony of his Scipio to be the actual case of early republic with no caution. In a time of antiquarian interest ignited by civic turmoil and following conservative nostalgia, it was more a reconstructive imagination of _mos miorum_ than a historical account. (cf. [[Areas/学业/文献库/罗马艺术与视觉文化/fasces/IACOBONI.pdf#page=6|Iacoboni, 2014, pp.289ff.]])

It's fairly probable that ridding the fasces of axes didn't exist as an established ritual in the time of Cicero, or at least earlier, but was more likely an anticipated display of regard for _res publica_.

Another coin could help us conclude this question with fair certainty. (RRC 403/1) In the near aftermath of the Social War, a coin, with the legend "Ita(lia) Ro(ma) (Con)cordi(a)", the two figures of Roma and Italy, as well as a whole lot of positive imageries like caduceus, cornucopia and handshake, nevertheless showed a fasces neither rid of its axe, nor lowered.

I suggest that we understand it in such light - Fasces might appear terrifying to slaves and provincials and thus conveyed more of violence and power of life and death. But to deserving citizens it was another deal. In Roman republican political culture, _honores_, meaning both honours and offices, were seen as a gift of recognition endowed by the _populus Romanus_, which necessitated the magistrates' _gratia_ and _officium_. ([[Areas/学业/文献库/罗马政治文化/共和晚期/Reconstructing the Roman Republic An Ancient Political Culture and Modern Research (Karl-J. Hölkeskamp  Henry Heitmann-Gordon) (z-library.sk, 1lib.sk, z-lib.sk).pdf#page=107|Hölkeskamp, 2010, p. 90]]) In a citizen's view, the _fasces_ signified such _gratia_ and _officium_, especially in the context of a minter as would-be magistrate propagating his ancestors' _honores_, though violence the fasces reminded of shouldn't be completely overlooked. ([[Areas/学业/文献库/罗马艺术与视觉文化/fasces/Marshall-SymbolsShowmanshipRoman-1984.pdf#page=16|Marshall, 1984, pp. 135-136]]) The fasces could thereby invoke a reciprocal relationship between magistrates and voters.

Also, in a City of Rome multi-ethnic and hierarchical, fasces as a reminder of franchise and citizenship further inspired pride in Roman identity and the ruling status of the Roman citizens themselves. And the coin of Italy and Rome could be understood in this light: the axed fasces were not intended to display Roman _imperium_ _over_ Italy, but that shared _with_ Italy, the Italians being fellow citizens as opposed to provincials. ([[Areas/学业/文献库/罗马政治文化/共和晚期/Reconstructing the Roman Republic An Ancient Political Culture and Modern Research (Karl-J. Hölkeskamp  Henry Heitmann-Gordon) (z-library.sk, 1lib.sk, z-lib.sk).pdf#page=106|Hölkeskamp, 2010, pp. 89-90]])

We could thereby conclude the axed fasces didn't necessarily appear terrifying to the Roman citizens since they presumed axes as bestowed to magistrates by themselves, and to be used against provincials and slaves whom they seldom showed sympathy.

The tomb of Servius Sulpicius Galba boasted a dozen of fasces, and telling from the axe handle tied to the bundle of rods, the fasces were axed. The tomb was situated in the _emporium_, between _Porticus Aemilia_ and _Horrea Galbana_, a place of considerable traffic already in 2nd. century BC. Sulpicius Galba was notorious, or famous, for his brutal treatment of the Lusitanians, due to which he was charged by tribune of the plebs Lucius Scribonius Libo and condemned by Cato the Censor. (Livy _Per_. 49.17–20, App. _Hisp_. 59–60) However, he was nevertheless acquitted and elected consul barely years later. When we put ourselves into the Roman voters' shoes, we can understand that Galba's axes didn't evoke terror, but instead pride and a sense of identity. (for the role language played in manifestation of Roman identity, cf. [[Areas/学业/文献库/罗马艺术与视觉文化/fasces/migration-mobility-and-language-contact-in-and-around-the-ancient-mediterranean-cambridge-classical-studies-9781108488440-9781108763943-1108488447_compress.pdf|Elder, 2020, pp. 292]]) Cicero's account fails here as it does in many other occasions.

###### III. Removal of Axes Found Its Way Into Imagery Representation

Despite all given examples, Cicero, being the most celebrated orator in the late republican and early principate era, was gaining recognition. A series of coins were minted by Lucius Livineius Regulus at Rome in the year of the battle of Philippi (RRC 494/26, RRC 494/27) which copied Brocchus' pattern, nonetheless removed the axes. Likewise, Julius Caesar used fasces rid of axes as part of his political propaganda. Fasces, unaxed, crossed with caduceus, appeared together with clasped hands and a sacrificial axe. (RRC 480/6) Caesar _Dictator Perpetus_ was justifying his dictatorship in the name of peace, piety and order, calling for concord.

In the years of the dying _res publica libera_ when axed _fasces_ were more and more applied to citizens, the meaning they invoked among the intended viewers changed substantially, and the ritual of removing the axes, whether invented or indeed once existed, found its way into imagery representation: It offered a way of displaying legitimacy and harnessing reconstructed ancient history to serve contemporary political agendas. And Octavian, whose career was full of illegality and thereby was in dire need of means of legitimisation, wouldn't have missed it: As Octavianus could betray Cicero effortlessly for the triumvirate, Augustus also praised him unstintingly as "_aner logios kai philopatris_" (Plut. _Cic._ 49.3), precisely as he was, working _ad_ _res publica restituta_. (cf. [[Areas/学业/文献库/罗马艺术与视觉文化/fasces/The Power of Images in the Age of Augustus (Thomas Spencer Jerome Lectures) (Paul Zanker) (z-library.sk, 1lib.sk, z-lib.sk).pdf#page=111|Zanker, 1990, p.100]]) He could then tolerate axed fasces to chop off Cicero's head, and stealing the latter's account of fasces as well, carefully ridding fasces of their axes as if he were another Publicola or Junius Brutus.

Examining the fasces on Ara Pacis can render us some insights. No Fasces on Ara Pacis were axed, and they were deliberately carved in low relief so that in contrast to high-relief figures in front, they would either be missed or blocked from view.

In the same frieze, we see what the princeps more willingly intended to propagate, in high relief and in front of all other figures, with Agrippa's eyes falling on it: _securis victimaria_. Augustus preferred the one more suitable for his role as an law-abiding citizen: he preferred to be a tribune or pontiff, a protector and benefactor of the Roman people, rather than a proconsul of all proconsuls with all legions in his clutches; He preferred to show _securis victimaria_ and appear a Roman both pious, humble, and dedicated to duty, rather than a holder of _imperium_ the number of whose lictors equalled that of the ancient kings. ([[Areas/学业/文献库/罗马艺术与视觉文化/fasces/The Power of Images in the Age of Augustus (Thomas Spencer Jerome Lectures) (Paul Zanker) (z-library.sk, 1lib.sk, z-lib.sk).pdf#page=138|Zanker, 1990, p.85-86, 127-128]]) But in the same time he was perfectly glad if such power (or put more precisely, _auctoritas_ instead of _potentia_) was felt, as long as it was veiled behind the façade of legitimacy. The representation of the axes as such was an appropriation of Cicero's prototype, and it was though such appropriation that the ritual manifested its meaning and became truly felt through visual propaganda.

###### IV Appropriations of the Appropriation?

The successful example of Augustus was followed by later emperors as well, creating a whole new range of stipulation of the axes. Here I try to provide some tentative examples; They're by no means conclusive.

The _Ara Pietatis Augustae_, dedicated by Claudius in 43 CE, followed Augustus' general line of presenting fasces: Lictors behind major figures, and display of fasces without axes. And on a silver Skyphos depicting Claudius' triumph in memory of his Britain campaigns, the lictors are strategically positioned behind the horses, ensuring the _fasces_ are physically blocked from the viewer's gaze.

Such concealment was by no means accidental, as a comparison with the later _Arch of Titus_ reveals. In the Flavian relief, although the lictors remain behind the horses, the sculptor deliberately spared space for a prominent axe to be featured. And another Flavian, Domitian, boasted _fasces_ even more proudly: In the panel A of Cancelleria Reliefs, arms of Roma, Domitian, and Minerva led to an axe, carved in high relief and pointing out threateningly. I propose tentatively the _Lex de Imperio Vespasiani_ as a possible explanation. Always under wraps as pontifex maximus, Augustus also veiled the brutality of his regime with unaxed fasces; But Vespasian, whose right to rule was for the first time formally codified in a single legal instrument by the senate, boasted axed fasces inside and outside the pomerium, as did almost all the following two Flavians and with Domitian reaching its peak. (For the role of manliness and military scenarios in Flavian ideology, cf. Shannon, 2014, pp.34-36, 41-42)

###### V. Conclusion

In summary, this investigation has shown that the "ritual of the unaxed fasces" was less a timeless Republican tradition and more a sophisticated piece of political branding. What began as a Ciceronian reconstructive imagination of the _mos maiorum_ was manipulated, appropriated and transformed by Augustus into a visual language of legitimacy — a way to reconcile absolute power with Republican sensibilities; and that appropriation in turn opened another new scope for further manipulation for the emperors to come.

Of course, this study has its limitations. More comprehensive interpretations combining visual data and literary sources are needed to perfect this inquiry, and the era of the Trajan and Hadrian when the fasces made a full-scale come back needs more detailed investigation.  Nonetheless, the evolution of this symbol reminds us that in Rome, the most powerful messages were often conveyed not by what was shown, but by what was carefully hidden, and all the manipulations and appropriations behind that.
