# the case of the tribune-elect
==**magistratus designatus**==: formally privatus, but resembling magistratus

## Cic. Verr. 1.37
- 2 points Cic. makes to justify his status as aedile-elect being more important than Hortensius' as consul-elect
	- he is more beneficient to the Roman People
	- he is more active as a magistrate-elect

## formal / symbolic status
- Terminology resembled that of an incumbent magistrate
- Resignation was legally identical to that of magistrates
- Names were entered in the *fasti* even if office was never actually assumed
- Oaths of loyalty had already been taken

## participation before office
- Could speak *ex superiore loco*
- Could allot provinces among themselves
- In the senate, voted in the class appropriate to their future office
- *Consules designati* could speak first in the senate

## practical functions and powers
- Could conduct preliminary negotiations with foreign ambassadors
- Could be asked by the senate to carry out actions immediately or after taking office
- ==Could conduct elections==
- ==Could issue binding edicts==: with postponed effect (after entering office), and possibly also with immediate effect

# Designati: A Focus of Political Communication
- Speeches in contiones
	- Pompeius as *designatus* made speeches in *contiones*, promising restoration of tribunician powers (Cic. *Verr.* 1.45)
	- Metellus Nepos and C. Memmius as tribune-designates also made important announcements in *contiones*
	- For the sources, the initiative of the *designatus* is what matters
- Preparation of bills
	- *Designati* could prepare legislation and involve other *privati* in this work (Cic. *Leg. Agr.* 2.11–12)
- Political weight of the title
	- Cicero explains Hortensius’ effectiveness in defending Verres through his status as *consul designatus* (Cic. *Verr.* 2.3.222; *Brut.* 319)
	- The very name and *maiestas* of a *consul designatus* was politically significant in 43 (*Phil.* 13.20)
- Houses as communication hubs
	- The houses of *designati* functioned as centers of political communication (Cic. *Verr.* 1.27; 2.3.45), possibly also with foreign representatives
	- Cicero contrasts an invitation to the house of *consules designati* with a summons to the house of incumbent consuls
- A point of attraction
	- A *designatus* could become a focal point in unofficial political communication
	- A large *conventus* gathered around the consul-designate Hortensius, making his words and even those of supporters widely known (Cic. *Verr.* 1.18)

# Designati: concluding points
- 1 A transitional status
	- Formally a *privatus*, without magisterial prerogatives (no immunity, no *ius edicendi*)
	- In practice, could take political initiative as if already in office
	- Sequence: *privatus* → *designatus* → *magistratus*
- 2 Competing with incumbents
	- Magistrates-elect, especially consuls-elect, could compete successfully with incumbent magistrates
	- Ancient authors depict them as acting like magistrates or even in their place – especially when incumbents were inactive or absent
- 3 Informal influence and *auctoritas*
	- Ancient authors stress the strong *auctoritas* of some *designati*
	- Even opponents acknowledged their influence
	- Narratives often omit other actors and use “legitimising” language: *edictum et litterae*, γράμματα, *vetare*, etc.
- 4 A consistent narrative pattern
	- The use of “legitimising” terminology did not depend on whether the author was favourable or hostile
	- *Designati* are likened to magistrates whenever they take initiative of a magisterial kind
	- Where no such initiative is present, no comparison is made – even if their action is central to the narrative
- 5 Using the expectation of office
	- Short intervals between election and office met longer political processes
	- *Designati* could use the expectation of imminent office:
		- promise changes to a future *edictum perpetuum*
		- threaten future use of *potestas*
		- address *contiones* in place of an absent incumbent
	- The same expectation could also constrain their choices, and rivals exploited these constraints
- 6 When opportunity arises
	- *Designati* became visible when incumbent magistrates failed to provide effective leadership
	- Their intervention offered a flexible and timely means of compensating for this deficit
	- Success depended on the timing of events (elections, entry into office, etc.) and the ability to act informally
- 7 A means of legitimising power
	- Used with varying success, the status of *designatus* had become, by the end of the Republic, a way to display and legitimise real political power
	- It particularly benefited those whose actual influence could not be contained within the formal magisterial system
